The Origin Story

In October 2022, Waseem Daher sent an email to two friends. The problem was simple. The solution was not.

Waseem Daher October 16, 2022

I have about 8,000 family photos. Total size ~40GB or so. I've organized almost all of them into folders by date/event, but about 1,300 remain to be sorted. Of those 1,300, many of them are duplicates of images I've already sorted, but they're not perfect duplicates because: sometimes they're the result of having scanned a print more than once, sometimes one is "the scan of the negative" and one is "the scan of the print."

The constraints made this interesting. These weren't digital camera burst shots. They were analog photographs that had been scanned multiple times, sometimes as negatives, sometimes as prints. The "same" photo could look dramatically different depending on the scanning hardware, orientation, color correction, and whether it was scanned from a print or a negative.

Waseem framed two sub-problems:

  1. Duplicate detection — find near-identical images across folders
  2. Similar scene detection — group thematically related photos

And he did the math on brute force:

Waseem Daher Doing #2 by hand is too slow — 8000² = 64,000,000 — if each comparison took only one second it would take me two years. My budget for this is probably like... ≤$1k, not $640k.

Ray He suggested perceptual hashing and CLIP-based scene descriptions. Keith Winstein, a Stanford CS professor, wished for a turnkey solution that didn't exist.

What Actually Worked in 2022

After trying imagehash (poor results) and CLIP interrogator (cool but impractical), Waseem landed on pHash — the perceptual hash library using DCT (Discrete Cosine Transform) to generate compact fingerprints of images.

Waseem Daher October 17, 2022

phash.org actually seems to work well on my three test cases (the DCT option). Of course, it didn't have Python bindings and I was totally unable to get it to build in Colab, but building it locally seems to actually sorta work. And the n² comparison is OK as long as the actual comparison itself is fast!

The result:

Waseem Daher October 18, 2022

I hash each image, and then I do the n² comparison over all hashes to compute the hamming distance between the hashes to score them on similarity. If it's < 10, they're basically in fact "the same image", if it's ~15 or so, sometimes they are, sometimes they're totally random.

It worked. Well enough, anyway. "Well enough to give the human (me) some manual tasks to do that I think are reasonably good uses of time."

Keith's reaction captured the state of the art perfectly:

Keith Winstein, Stanford CS October 18, 2022

I don't have a lot to add to this, but I'm sorry the answer isn't just "if you don't want to DIY this, you can put everything in Google Photos [or FOSS Gallery2, or Adobe Whatever, or some other photo-archiving software] and it will dependably eliminate duplicates and cluster similar events together for you." :-/

And then came The Prophecy:

November 2023

2023–2024

Meta's DINOv2 (self-supervised ViT) produces excellent visual embeddings without any labels. Immich ships CLIP-based smart search. FAISS makes ANN search accessible. The building blocks are all free and open-source.
2025–2026
Basically Solved
Purpose-built tools like photo-dedup (DINOv2 + MUSIQ + face-pose), Mirror of Maya (DINOv2 + FAISS, F1 > 0.93), and swarm-of-agents-photo-gaze (DINOv2 + Qdrant) solve Waseem's exact problem. Cost: $0. Time: an afternoon.

The key insight isn't just that individual comparisons got better — it's that you no longer need n² comparisons at all. Vector databases with approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) algorithms like HNSW turn the search from "compare everything to everything" into "find the closest neighbors in a pre-built index." For 8,000 images, this means ~8,000 lookups against a FAISS index instead of ~32 million pairwise comparisons.

Step 1: Load. Read each image, resize to 224×224 (or 518×518 for DINOv2-large), normalize.

Step 2: Embed. Pass each image through a vision transformer. DINOv2 ViT-B/14 produces a 768-dimensional vector that captures visual and semantic features. On an RTX 3060, this processes ~200 images/second. For 8,000 images: about 40 seconds.

Step 3: Index. Build a FAISS IndexFlatIP (inner product) or IndexIVFFlat index over the embeddings. For 8,000 vectors at 768 dimensions, the index fits in ~24MB of RAM. Building it takes milliseconds.

Step 4: Search. For each of the 1,300 unsorted images, query the index for the top-5 nearest neighbors. With an IVF index, each query takes ~1ms. Total: ~1.3 seconds. Cosine similarity > 0.95 = very likely duplicate. 0.85–0.95 = similar scene. < 0.85 = different photo.

Step 5: Review. Only the ambiguous cases (0.85–0.95 similarity) need human review. For scanned family photos, this is typically 5–15% of matches. The rest are either obvious duplicates or clearly different.

What's Still Hard

Waseem's problem included a genuinely tricky edge case: negatives vs. prints. When you scan a photographic negative and a print of the same photo, you get:

  • Inverted luminance (negative is inverted)
  • Different color profiles (negative film base is orange)
  • Different dynamic range and contrast curves
  • Different crops from the scanner bed
  • Different dust/scratch artifacts

Modern embeddings handle most of this remarkably well — DINOv2 and CLIP learn features that are invariant to color inversion and contrast changes because they've seen millions of augmented training images. But there are still genuinely hard cases:

  • Photo of a photo: A digital photo of a physical print introduces perspective distortion, glare, ambient lighting, and moiré patterns. Embeddings catch the subject similarity but the distance can be high enough to miss at strict thresholds.
  • Extreme fading: Severely faded prints from the 1960s–1970s can shift so far in color space that even learned features struggle. The content is there but buried under decades of chemical decay.
  • Mixed generations: Original → photocopy → scan → JPEG → screenshot → re-upload. Each generation degrades differently. Perceptual hashes fail by generation 3. Embeddings usually hold to generation 4–5.

For Waseem's specific collection (family photos, scanned prints and negatives), DINOv2 embeddings with a cosine threshold of ~0.80–0.85 would likely catch 90%+ of the cross-media duplicates that pHash missed in 2022.
